Neurology Outpatient Clinic                                                                Alex Chen, 47, male
Letter to referring physician. Copy given to the patient at his request.                      To: Dr. P. Raman


Diagnosis: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), limb onset, with early bulbar and respiratory
involvement

Dear Dr. Raman,

I saw Alex and his partner Jordan today to go through his results, and I am sorry to say that the
diagnosis is ALS. I spent the appointment with them and the clinic social worker, and they will return in
two weeks to talk further. He asked that this letter be written in plain language and that he be given a
copy, which I have done.

How the diagnosis was made. ALS is diagnosed on the clinical picture, not on any single test. Alex has
progressive weakness with signs of both upper motor neuron damage (brisk reflexes, clonus, a
Hoffmann sign, an upgoing plantar, spastic speech) and lower motor neuron damage (wasting,
fasciculations, low motor amplitudes, denervation on EMG) in the same regions, now spread across all
four: bulbar, cervical, thoracic and lumbosacral. Sensation is normal. The MRI excludes the structural
conditions that can mimic this, the bloods exclude the treatable ones, and the EMG shows the process
is active and widespread. He meets the Gold Coast criteria without ambiguity. In retrospect he probably
met them a year ago.

What ALS is. A progressive loss of the motor neurons that drive voluntary muscle, in the brain and the
spinal cord. The muscle itself is healthy; it wastes because the nerve supply dies. Sensation, eye
movements, bladder and bowel are spared. Thinking is usually spared, though about half of patients
develop some change in word-finding or executive function, and Alex's reduced verbal fluency is of that
kind. The cause in his case, as in about nine of ten cases, is unknown.

Treatment. There is no cure. Two drugs have been shown to slow progression modestly: riluzole, which I
have started today, and edaravone, which we will discuss. A third drug, tofersen, is the first treatment to
act on a cause of ALS. It is an antisense oligonucleotide that silences a faulty SOD1 gene, and because it
cannot cross the blood-brain barrier it is given by lumbar puncture every month. Health Canada
authorised it with conditions in February 2025. It applies to the roughly two per cent of patients who
carry a SOD1 mutation. Alex's genetic panel was negative, so, like most people with this disease, he has
no treatment directed at its cause. Beyond drugs, what has been shown to lengthen life is care at a
multidisciplinary clinic, keeping his weight up, and breathing support at night once it is needed.

What to expect. His progression over two years has been steady rather than rapid, which is in his
favour. The pattern will continue: the right hand and leg will weaken further and the left side will follow;
speech, swallowing and breathing have begun to be involved and will progress. I have been honest with
them that the median survival from symptom onset is two to four years, that the range is wide, and that
the decisions ahead (a feeding tube, ventilation at night, how he wants to communicate when speech
is difficult) are better made early. I have referred him to the regional ALS clinic, which will see him every
three months, and to ALS Canada for equipment and support.

He intends to keep teaching this year. I see no reason he should not.

Dr. Hannah Weiss-Okafor, MD, FRCPC
Neurologist, neuromuscular disease

The tracks
Choose one track to focus on.


1. Detection and Diagnostics
  Alex waited two years for a diagnosis, through four clinicians and an
  operation that did nothing.
  In Canada the wait is typically 15 to 27 months depending on the province.
  Since diagnosis his disease is followed with a questionnaire every three
  months.
  Build something that detects the disease earlier or predicts how it will
  progress. Use the datasets on the next page or find your own.


2. Therapeutics and Delivery
  The one drug that acts on a cause of ALS applies to about two in a
  hundred patients. Alex is not one of them.
  The two other approved drugs add months. More than fifty late-stage trials
  have failed since riluzole was approved in 1995.
  Getting anything into a spinal motor neuron is itself an unsolved problem.
  Propose a therapy, or a way of delivering one, that could slow the disease
  or protect the neurons that remain. Say what it would mean for Alex now
  and for someone where he was in 2024.


3. Wearable Devices and Quality of Life
  He is strong in the morning and weak by the afternoon. He teaches in a
  classroom where he needs to be heard, he is alone at home until 4:30, and
  he has fallen on the stairs.
  His right hand has a few seconds of pinch left. His left is starting to go.
  Most assistive devices end up in a drawer because nobody asked the
  person what they wanted.
  Design a device or tool that lets Alex keep one thing that matters to him as
  his abilities change. The MLH hardware lab is open all weekend if you want
  to build.
